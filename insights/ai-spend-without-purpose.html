AI spend without a purpose

November 10, 2026 · Thomas Young

Some companies are now spending $1 million or $2 million a month on AI. Tools, seats, tokens, pilots that never quite end. Ask the people running those budgets what they're getting back and the honest answer is usually some version of "hard to tell."

That answer should worry a CFO more than the number itself. A big bill tied to a clear outcome is an investment. A big bill tied to nothing in particular is a habit.

How the spend gets ahead of the purpose

Most AI budgets didn't start as budgets. They started as a few licenses for a team that wanted to try something, then an API key for an engineer building a prototype, then an enterprise agreement because procurement wanted one contract instead of forty. Each step made sense on its own. Nobody sat down and decided the company would spend seven figures a month on AI. It accumulated.

Token costs make this worse because they scale with use, and use has nothing to do with value. A workflow that calls a model a thousand times a day costs the same whether it saves someone four hours or produces output nobody reads. The invoice shows volume. It says nothing about whether the work mattered.

So the spend climbs and the purpose stays vague. "We're investing in AI" becomes the purpose, which is another way of saying there isn't one.

The bill is the part everyone can see. What's broken is rarely what's visible, and here the real gap is that nobody wrote down what success looks like. That gap shows up at the worst time: when the board wants an AI plan and the honest answer is a list of pilots and an invoice.

Why the return is hard to see

When leaders say AI ROI is hard to measure, they're usually right, but often for a fixable reason. Nobody defined what return meant before the money went out.

A tool bought to "improve productivity" has no finish line. A tool bought to cut invoice processing from five days to two has one. The second goal is narrower and less exciting in a board deck. It's also the only one you can check.

There's a second problem. Usage numbers get reported as results: seats activated and tokens consumed. Those numbers prove people are using the tools. They don't prove the business is better off. A team can be enthusiastic AI users and still deliver the same output at the same cost as last year.

Start with an inventory of what you're paying for

When we look at an AI program, the first job is to assess what's going on before anyone decides whether to spend more or less. That means a plain inventory: every AI tool and contract (API accounts included), what each one costs per month, and who owns it.

Expect gaps in the ownership column. Any line item without an owner who can explain what it's for is the first place to look.

Next to each line, write down the business result it's supposed to move, stated as an outcome someone outside IT would recognize, like a faster month-end close or fewer escalated support tickets. If nobody can name one, that line is a candidate to pause.

Tie each dollar to one number

Once the inventory exists, the next step is to align the teams who own the spend with the teams who own the result. AI budgets often sit with IT or a central innovation group, while the outcomes they're meant to change sit in finance or operations. When those groups don't share a target, one team measures the cost and another measures the value, and nobody connects the two.

Pick one number per use case. Agree on the baseline before the tool goes live, or reconstruct it now if it already has. Review that number monthly next to what the use case costs. That's the whole system. It isn't sophisticated, and it doesn't need to be.

Spend more where it works

Having a purpose doesn't mean spending less on AI. Some use cases in that inventory will show a clear return once someone measures them, and those deserve more money. The point is to know which ones they are, and to accelerate the delivery of the work that's paying off instead of spreading budget evenly across everything with AI in the name.

Companies spending $1 million or $2 million a month on AI can afford to answer the return question properly. If yours can't answer it yet, start with the inventory. It's a few weeks of unglamorous work, and it turns "hard to tell" into an answer before next year's bill arrives.
